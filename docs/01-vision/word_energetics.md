## Premise

Copy potency is physical, not decorative. A sentence either carries or it doesn't, and the difference is not style. Sasha has named three sources of this, each still awaiting his full articulation:

1. **The energy behind words.** The same sentence carries or doesn't depending on the state it was written from. Two people can write the identical line and only one version lands, because the words are a carrier signal for the state of the writer, not just a container for meaning.
2. **Functional names.** Technology 128 (`phase_shift_technology_library.md`): the name states the result. A name that says what the thing does to you needs no persuasion sitting underneath it.
3. **PIE etymology as civilizational cheat codes.** The Proto-Indo-European roots of words carry their original operational meaning. Copy that aligns with the root lands deeper than copy that fights it — the root already did the work of pointing at the true mechanism, thousands of years before the copywriter arrived.

## First law (from today's work)

**Pointing beats abstraction.**

Abstract deixis — "there's something valuable here," "the pattern underneath it" — is weaker than pointing at the actual phenomenon in the person's own life, in their own words. Every "it / this / there" in a piece of copy is a slot where a concrete lived detail should stand instead.

Example pair, from the quiz result, Day 139:

> "You already know there's something valuable here. What you can't see yet is the pattern underneath it."

This is strong structure. Its next octave is filling the slots with the taker's own answers, so "there" and "it" become the specific thing they actually said.
